Sales Tax and 1099-K for Online Sellers: A Plain-English 2026 Guide

By The LedgerLane Team · Updated July 2026 · 8 min read
This is a plain-English overview to help you get organized — it is not tax advice. Tax rules change and depend on your state and situation. Always confirm the current figures and requirements with the IRS and a qualified tax professional.

Taxes are where a lot of sellers freeze. The forms have intimidating names, the thresholds keep changing, and the fear of getting it wrong makes it easy to do nothing until April. But the actual moving parts are few, and most of the stress comes from disorganized records, not the rules themselves. Here's the map.

The 1099-K, demystified

A 1099-K is an informational form that payment platforms (Etsy, PayPal, Shopify Payments, Stripe) send you and the IRS reporting how much they processed for you during the year. It is not a bill and not a new tax — it's a report. Your job is to make sure the income you report matches what these forms say.

The reporting threshold has been in flux. The IRS has been phasing toward a low $600 threshold, with transition years at higher amounts. Practically, assume you may receive a 1099-K even for a modest side income, and — importantly — you owe tax on business profit whether or not you receive a form. The form doesn't create the obligation; earning the income does. Check the current-year threshold before you file.

Sales tax vs. income tax — two different animals

These get confused constantly. They're unrelated:

Income taxSales tax
Who it's onYou (on your profit)The buyer (you collect & remit)
Based onNet profit for the yearThe sale price, by location
Who often handles itYou, via Schedule CUsually the marketplace, for you

The good news for most marketplace sellers: marketplace facilitator laws mean platforms like Etsy and Amazon are required to calculate, collect, and remit sales tax on your behalf in most states. You generally don't touch it for those sales. Where it gets real is your own website and in-person sales — there, collecting and remitting can be your responsibility, depending on your state and nexus. Confirm your state's rules.

Income tax: Schedule C and quarterly estimates

If you sell as a sole proprietor (most solo sellers do), your business income lands on Schedule C, attached to your personal return. You report revenue, subtract legitimate business expenses (including COGS and fees), and pay income tax plus self-employment tax on the profit.

Because no employer is withholding tax for you, the IRS generally expects quarterly estimated payments if you'll owe a meaningful amount for the year. A simple, safe habit: set aside a percentage of every profitable sale — many solo sellers park 25–30% of profit in a separate account — so the quarterly payment is money you already have, not a scramble. Confirm the right set-aside rate for your bracket with a professional.

The Schedule C categories are the same buckets you should already be tracking for profit. Track cleanly all year (see our profit-tracking guide) and tax filing becomes copying totals — not reconstructing twelve months from memory.

Deductions you shouldn't miss

  • COGS — materials, direct labor, production supplies
  • Marketplace & payment fees — every listing, transaction, and processing fee
  • Shipping — postage and shipping supplies
  • Software & subscriptions — the tools you run the shop with
  • Home office & mileage — if you qualify; rules are specific, so verify
  • Marketing — ads, promoted listings, product photography costs

Every one of these is money you don't pay tax on — but only if you recorded it. Undocumented deductions are lost deductions.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 1099-K threshold for 2026?

The IRS has been phasing toward a $600 reporting threshold, with higher amounts in transition years, so many sellers will receive a 1099-K even for modest income. Confirm the current-year figure before filing. Remember: you owe tax on business profit whether or not you receive the form.

Do I have to collect sales tax on Etsy?

In most US states, no — marketplace facilitator laws require Etsy and similar platforms to calculate, collect, and remit sales tax for you on marketplace sales. Your own website and in-person sales may be your responsibility, depending on your state and nexus. Always verify your state's rules.

How much should I set aside for taxes as an online seller?

Many solo sellers set aside 25–30% of their profit in a separate account to cover income and self-employment tax, paid via quarterly estimates. The right rate depends on your bracket and situation — confirm it with a tax professional. This is organizational guidance, not tax advice.
