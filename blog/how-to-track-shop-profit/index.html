How to Track Profit for Your Etsy or Shopify Shop (Without an Accountant)

By The LedgerLane Team · Updated July 2026 · 6 min read

Plenty of shops know their revenue to the dollar and have no idea what their profit is. Revenue is the number the platform shows you; profit is the number that's actually yours. The gap between them — fees, materials, shipping, software, the booth fee for that craft fair — is where most small sellers lose the plot. You don't need an accountant or accounting software to close that gap. You need a habit and four numbers.

The four numbers that matter

  1. Revenue — total money in from sales, before anything is taken out.
  2. Cost of goods sold (COGS) — what the things you sold cost to make: materials, and the direct labor and packaging tied to them.
  3. Operating expenses — everything else it takes to run the shop: platform fees, shipping, software, ads, booth fees, a share of supplies.
  4. Net profit — revenue − COGS − operating expenses. This is the one that pays you.
If you only ever track one derived number, track net profit margin = net profit ÷ revenue. A shop doing $3,000 a month at a 12% margin ($360 profit) is in more trouble than one doing $1,200 at 35% ($420). Revenue flatters; margin tells the truth.

A monthly system you'll actually keep up

Fancy systems get abandoned. This one takes about twenty minutes a month:

  1. Export your sales. Etsy, Shopify, and most platforms let you download a monthly orders CSV. That's your revenue and your fees in one file.
  2. Log expenses as they happen, or once a month. Keep a single running list: date, amount, category, note. A recurring calendar reminder beats a perfect app you never open.
  3. Categorize into a fixed set of buckets. Use the same categories every month (materials, fees, shipping, software, ads, other). Consistency is what makes months comparable — and makes tax time painless.
  4. Read the four numbers. Revenue, COGS, expenses, net profit. Compare to last month. That's the whole review.

Categorize once, benefit twice

The categories you track profit with are almost exactly the categories the IRS Schedule C asks for. Track cleanly all year and tax season becomes copying totals, not reconstructing a year from memory. (More on that in our sales tax and 1099-K guide.) The reverse is also true: shops that only think about categories in April make worse pricing decisions for the other eleven months, because they never see which products actually earn.

Find your real winners and losers

Once profit is tracked per product — not just per shop — patterns jump out. The bestseller you're proud of might be your thinnest margin. A quiet listing might be quietly carrying the shop. You can't see any of that from revenue alone. This is where a dashboard earns its keep: paste the month's transactions and it sorts revenue, expenses, and profit, then ranks your top products so you know where to spend your limited making hours.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between revenue and profit?

Revenue is all the money that comes in from sales. Profit is what's left after you subtract the cost of making what you sold (COGS) and the cost of running the shop (fees, shipping, software, ads). A shop can have high revenue and almost no profit if its margins are thin.

How often should I track my shop's profit?

Monthly is the sweet spot for most small sellers — frequent enough to catch problems early, rare enough to stay a 20-minute habit. Export your platform's monthly orders CSV, log expenses into fixed categories, and read your four numbers: revenue, COGS, expenses, net profit.

Do I need accounting software like QuickBooks?

Not to start. A well-structured spreadsheet handles profit tracking, product-level margins, and tax categorization for most solo sellers, and costs nothing per month. Dedicated software makes sense once you have payroll, inventory across many locations, or an accountant who requests it.
