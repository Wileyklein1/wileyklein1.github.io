The Reorder Point Formula: Never Run Out of Materials Mid-Order

By The LedgerLane Team · Updated July 2026 · 6 min read

There are two ways to run out of a material: not enough warning, or no system to give the warning. Both end the same way — an order you can't fill, a supplier rush-shipping at a premium, or cash tied up in a panic buy. The fix is one small number per material: the reorder point, the stock level that means 'order more now.' Here's how to calculate it.

What a reorder point is

A reorder point is the quantity at which you place a new order so that fresh stock arrives just as you're about to run out. It accounts for two things: how fast you use the material, and how long the supplier takes to deliver. Get it right and you never sit idle waiting on materials — and never over-stock cash into a shelf, either.

The formula

Reorder point = (average daily usage × lead time in days) + safety stock
  • Average daily usage — how much of the material you consume per day, on average.
  • Lead time — days from placing an order to having it in hand and usable.
  • Safety stock — a buffer for the bad case: a demand spike or a slow supplier. This is what keeps a normal wobble from becoming a stockout.

A worked example

You make candles and use fragrance oil at about 8 oz per day. Your supplier's lead time is 7 days. You want a safety stock of 20 oz in case a big order lands or shipping slips:

Reorder point = (8 oz/day × 7 days) + 20 oz = 56 + 20 = 76 oz

So the moment your fragrance oil drops to 76 oz, you order more. New stock arrives around the time you'd have hit your 20 oz buffer — never zero, never a panic. Do this once per key material and 'we're out of X' stops happening.

No safety stock is the most common mistake. If you set the reorder point to exactly usage × lead time, you're betting nothing ever goes wrong — no busy week, no late shipment. Safety stock is cheap insurance against the day both happen at once.

Setting your safety stock

Safety stock is a judgment call, and it scales with two things: how variable your demand is, and how unreliable your supplier is. A material you burn through in unpredictable bursts, from a supplier who's sometimes late, deserves a bigger buffer. A steady-use material from a next-day supplier needs almost none. A simple starting point: set safety stock to cover a few extra days of your average usage, then adjust up if you ever cut it close.

Why this connects to your cash

Reorder points aren't only about not running out — they're about not over-buying, either. Every dollar of material sitting on a shelf is a dollar not in your account (see cash flow for sellers). A good reorder point holds just enough: enough to never stop production, not so much that your cash is frozen as inventory. It's the middle path between 'out of stock' and 'over-stocked.'

Frequently asked questions

What is the reorder point formula?

Reorder point = (average daily usage × lead time in days) + safety stock. When a material's stock drops to that number, you place a new order so fresh stock arrives before you run out. Safety stock is a buffer that protects against demand spikes and slow suppliers.

How much safety stock should I keep?

Safety stock scales with how variable your demand is and how unreliable your supplier is — more of both means a bigger buffer. A simple starting point is enough to cover a few extra days of average usage, then adjust upward if you ever cut it close to running out.

Why not just reorder when I run low?

Because 'low' is a feeling, not a number, and it usually arrives too late — after a busy week has already drained the material. A calculated reorder point accounts for how fast you use the material and how long delivery takes, so you order at exactly the right moment: never idle, never over-stocked.
