LLC vs Sole Proprietor for Online Sellers: Which Should You Choose?

By The LedgerLane Team · Updated July 2026 · 7 min read
This is a plain-English overview to help you get organized — it is not legal or tax advice. Business structure rules vary by state and situation. Confirm the right choice for you with a qualified attorney or tax professional before you file anything.

'Do I need an LLC?' is one of the most common — and most over-thought — questions new online sellers ask. The honest answer for most people starting out is: you're probably already a business structure without doing anything, and forming an LLC is a decision about risk and growth, not a requirement to sell. Here's what the two options actually mean, so you can decide on purpose instead of out of anxiety.

You're a sole proprietor by default

The moment you start selling with the intent to make money, you're a sole proprietor in the eyes of the IRS — no forms, no fee, nothing to set up. Your business income and expenses flow onto a Schedule C attached to your personal tax return, and business and personal are legally the same entity.

  • Pros: zero cost, zero setup, simplest possible taxes and paperwork.
  • Cons: no liability protection — if the business is sued or owes a debt, your personal assets (savings, car, potentially your home) are exposed.

What an LLC changes

A limited liability company creates a legal wall between 'you' and 'the business.' Its headline benefit is right in the name: limited liability.

  • Liability protection — in general, a lawsuit or debt against the business reaches only the business's assets, not your personal ones (as long as you keep finances genuinely separate).
  • Credibility — some wholesale buyers, suppliers, and banks take an LLC more seriously.
  • Costs and upkeep — a state filing fee (often ~$50–$300) plus possible annual fees and reports. More paperwork than a sole prop, though still manageable.
  • Taxes, usually unchanged at first — a single-member LLC is taxed as a sole proprietor by default (still Schedule C), so forming one doesn't automatically raise or lower your taxes.
A common myth is that you need an LLC to 'save on taxes.' By default a single-member LLC is taxed exactly like a sole proprietor. The main reason to form one is liability protection, not a tax cut — though at higher income an S-corp election (a separate step) can change the math. Ask a professional.

A simple way to decide

Your situationOften reasonable
Just starting, low volume, low-risk productsSole proprietor — keep it simple
Growing income, personal assets to protectConsider an LLC
Products with real liability risk (skincare, food, kids' items)Lean toward an LLC sooner
Signing wholesale contracts / taking on debtLLC adds a protective layer

You can absolutely start as a sole proprietor and form an LLC later when the business is earning enough that the protection is worth the cost and paperwork. Many sellers do exactly that. There's no penalty for starting simple.

Whatever you choose, keep clean books

Here's what actually matters more than the letters after your business name on day one: keeping business and personal money separate and your records clean. A separate bank account, categorized income and expenses, and set-aside taxes protect you far more reliably than a rushed LLC with sloppy books. (In fact, mixing personal and business funds can undermine the liability protection an LLC is supposed to give you.)

Frequently asked questions

Do I need an LLC to sell on Etsy or online?

No. The moment you sell with intent to profit you're a sole proprietor by default — no forms or fees required — and you can legally sell that way. Forming an LLC is an optional decision about liability protection and growth, not a requirement to open a shop. Many sellers start as a sole proprietor and form an LLC later. This is general information, not legal advice.

Does an LLC lower my taxes?

Usually not by itself. A single-member LLC is taxed as a sole proprietor by default — the same Schedule C — so forming one doesn't automatically change your taxes. Its main benefit is liability protection. At higher income levels an S-corp election (a separate step) can change the tax math, so consult a tax professional about your specific situation.

When should an online seller switch from sole proprietor to LLC?

Consider it when your income grows enough that you have personal assets worth protecting, when you sell products with real liability risk (skincare, food, children's items), or when you start signing wholesale contracts or taking on debt. There's no penalty for starting as a sole proprietor and forming an LLC later once the protection is worth the cost — verify the specifics with a professional.
