How to Price Handmade Products in 2026 (The Formula That Keeps You in Profit)

By The LedgerLane Team · Updated July 2026 · 7 min read

Most handmade sellers set prices the same way: glance at what a competitor charges, round to something that feels okay, and hope. Then a busy month arrives, the orders pile up — and somehow the bank account barely moves. The culprit is almost always the same. The price never paid for the maker's own time.

Pricing is not a vibe. It's a short piece of arithmetic. Here is the whole thing, in plain English, with real numbers.

Start with your true unit cost

Before you can price anything, you need to know what one finished item actually costs you to make. That's three buckets, not one:

  • Materials — everything that ends up in the product or ships with it: raw materials, packaging, labels, the little thank-you card. Count the ribbon.
  • Labor — your time, valued at a real hourly wage. If you'd pay an employee $20/hour to do this, your time is worth $20/hour too. Twelve minutes of assembly is $4.00 of labor, not $0.
  • Overhead — the costs that don't belong to one item but are real all the same: tools wearing out, studio space, software, the electricity your kiln drinks. Spread it across a month of output as a small per-item amount.
The single most common pricing mistake is leaving labor at zero. "The materials only cost me $5" is how a maker ends up working for free. If you don't pay yourself in the price, no one will.

The formula

Once you have a true unit cost, pricing is two multiplications:

Base cost = materials + labor + overhead
Wholesale price = base cost × 2
Retail price = wholesale × 2  (often called "keystone" pricing)

The doubling isn't arbitrary. The first ×2 covers the costs a wholesale buyer expects you to absorb and leaves you a margin. The second ×2 is the retail markup that lets a shop — or you, selling direct — make money too. When you sell direct at retail, you simply keep the wholesale margin and the retail margin. That's the reward for doing your own selling.

A worked example

Say you make soy candles. For one candle:

Cost bucketAmount
Materials (wax, wick, jar, fragrance, label, box)$4.20
Labor (15 min pour + finishing @ $20/hr)$5.00
Overhead (share of studio, tools, utilities)$1.30
Base cost$10.50

Now apply the formula: wholesale = $10.50 × 2 = $21. Retail = $21 × 2 = $42. If $42 feels high for your market, that's useful information — it means your costs are high (probably labor time), not that you should simply charge less and eat the difference. Fix the cost, not the price.

Then subtract the fees — before you celebrate

Your retail price is not your profit. On a marketplace, a slice of every sale goes to listing fees, transaction fees, and payment processing. A $42 candle on Etsy might net closer to $36 after fees, and less again once you factor the shipping the platform charges fees on. If you priced right up front, that's fine — the margin absorbs it. If you priced thin, fees are what turn a "sale" into a wash. We break the fee math down in our guide to how Etsy fees actually work.

Three mistakes that quietly kill margins

  1. Pricing off competitors instead of costs. Their costs aren't yours. Maybe they buy materials at 10× your volume. Copying their price copies their economics, which you don't have.
  2. Never raising prices. Materials creep up every year. If your price is frozen at what felt right two years ago, your margin has been shrinking the whole time. Review prices at least annually.
  3. Discounting off a price that was already thin. A 20% "flash sale" on a product with a 25% margin leaves you almost nothing. Know your margin before you know your discount.

Frequently asked questions

What margin should a handmade product aim for?

As a rule of thumb, aim to keep at least 40–50% gross margin at retail after materials, labor, and fees. Below ~30% you have very little room for discounts, returns, or a bad month. The right number depends on your category and volume — the point is to choose it on purpose, not discover it at tax time.

Do I really need to charge for my own labor?

Yes. If you don't build your time into the price, your "profit" is really just unpaid wages you handed back to the customer. Pick an hourly rate you'd be willing to pay someone else for the same work, and include it in every unit cost.

How do I price for wholesale versus retail?

Wholesale is typically your base cost doubled; retail is wholesale doubled again (keystone). Selling direct at retail lets you keep both margins. If a wholesale buyer wants a lower price, the room has to come from lower costs or higher volume — never from erasing your margin.
