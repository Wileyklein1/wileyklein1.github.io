How to Price Products for Wholesale (Without Losing Money on Every Order)

By The LedgerLane Team · Updated July 2026 · 7 min read

A boutique emails asking to stock your product. It feels like you've made it — until you realize they expect to pay half your retail price, and you have no idea whether half your price still leaves you any profit. Price wholesale wrong and every big, exciting order quietly loses you money. Price it right and wholesale becomes the most predictable revenue you have. The difference is a formula and a few rules.

Wholesale is not "retail, discounted"

The most dangerous way to set a wholesale price is to take your retail price and knock a percentage off until it feels generous. That anchors wholesale to a number (retail) that was never built for it. Wholesale pricing works the other way around — it's built up from cost, and retail is built up from wholesale:

Base cost = materials + labor + overhead
Wholesale price = base cost × 2
Retail (MSRP) = wholesale × 2  (keystone)

That first doubling is your wholesale margin — the profit you make selling to a shop. The second is the retailer's margin, the room they need to make money reselling it. When you know your true base cost, both prices fall out of it, and you can see immediately whether a wholesale order is worth taking.

If you can't sell profitably at 2× your base cost, the problem isn't wholesale — it's that your base cost is too high or your retail price was always too thin. Wholesale simply exposes a margin problem that was already there. Fix the cost, not the wholesale discount.

A worked example

Say a candle costs you $10.50 to make (materials + labor + overhead). Here's how the two prices stack up:

LineAmount
Base cost (materials + labor + overhead)$10.50
Wholesale price (base × 2)$21.00
Suggested retail / MSRP (wholesale × 2)$42.00
Your profit per unit at wholesale$10.50

At wholesale you still make $10.50 per candle — you've simply traded the retail margin for volume and no selling effort. The shop does the selling; you ship a case. That's the trade wholesale offers: less profit per unit, far more units, and none of your marketing time per sale.

The rules that keep wholesale profitable

  1. Set a minimum order quantity (MOQ). Wholesale only makes sense at volume — a case, a dozen, a $150 minimum. An MOQ makes the lower per-unit price pay off in batch efficiency and one shipment instead of twelve.
  2. Never wholesale below your base cost × 2. That's your floor. If a buyer pushes under it, the room has to come from lower costs or a bigger order — never from erasing your margin.
  3. Keep retail price consistent everywhere. If you sell direct at retail and supply shops, don't undercut your wholesale partners on your own site — they'll stop reordering. Your MSRP protects them and you.
  4. Charge for setup on custom or private-label work. Custom colors, custom packaging, and a retailer's label are real costs; bill them separately rather than burying them in the unit price.
  5. Set clear terms up front. Payment (many makers require payment before shipping for new accounts), lead time, and who pays freight. Ambiguity is where wholesale relationships sour.

Line sheets and lead time

Wholesale buyers expect a line sheet — a clean one-page catalog with product photos, wholesale prices, MSRP, MOQs, and your terms. It signals you're a real supplier, not a hobbyist doing them a favor. Equally important is honest lead time: a shop planning a season needs to know whether a reorder takes one week or six, because your reliability is what earns the next order.

Know your cost cold before you quote

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate a wholesale price?

Build it up from your true cost, not down from retail. Add materials, labor, and overhead to get your base cost, then multiply by two for the wholesale price. Retail (MSRP) is typically wholesale doubled again. This keystone structure leaves both you and the retailer a margin — the retailer needs room to resell profitably, which is why wholesale is roughly half of retail.

Why is wholesale usually half of retail?

Because the retailer needs their own margin to make money reselling your product, cover their rent and staff, and take on the risk of stocking it. If you priced wholesale at 80% of retail, no shop could profit from carrying you. Keystone pricing (wholesale = half of retail) is the long-standing convention that leaves both sides a workable margin.

Should I offer wholesale if my margins are already thin?

Not until you fix the margin. Wholesale roughly halves your per-unit profit, so if retail barely clears your costs, wholesale will lose money on every order. First get your base cost accurate and your retail margin healthy; then wholesale (at base cost × 2, with a minimum order quantity) becomes profitable volume rather than a loss leader.
