What Profit Margin Should a Small Business Have? (Real Benchmarks for Sellers)

By The LedgerLane Team · Updated July 2026 · 7 min read

"Am I doing okay?" is the question behind every margin search. You're selling, money is moving, but you have no benchmark for whether the profit you keep is healthy or quietly heading for trouble. The honest answer has two parts: which margin you're actually asking about, and what's normal for a business like yours. Let's give you both — plus what to do if your number is too low.

Gross vs. net margin — don't confuse them

"Profit margin" means two very different numbers, and mixing them up is how sellers either panic or get complacent for no reason:

Gross marginNet margin
Formula(Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue(Revenue − ALL costs) ÷ Revenue
MeasuresProfit on the product itselfProfit the business actually keeps
Leaves outFees, shipping, software, ads, taxesNothing — it's the bottom line
Typical for sellers50–70%10–25%

Gross margin tells you whether each product is worth making; net margin tells you whether the business is worth running. A product with a 65% gross margin can still net you almost nothing if fees, shipping, and ads eat the rest. When someone quotes a margin, always ask which one — the two can differ by 40 points.

Net margin is the truth-teller. A shop doing $4,000/month at a 10% net margin keeps $400. A shop doing $1,800/month at 25% keeps $450 — and with far less work and risk. Revenue flatters; net margin is what actually pays you.

Realistic benchmarks for small sellers

Margins vary by industry, but for solo product businesses these are useful goalposts for net margin:

  • Below 10% — fragile. One bad month, a price hike from a supplier, or a wave of returns can wipe out your profit. Treat this as a warning light, not a resting state.
  • 10–20% — solid for a product business with physical goods, shipping, and marketplace fees. Most healthy handmade and e-commerce shops live here.
  • 20–30%+ — strong. Common for digital products, low-material-cost goods, or shops with real pricing power and an efficient operation.

Aim for a healthy gross margin first — 50% or more at retail after materials and labor — because gross margin is the raw material every other cost is paid out of. If gross margin is thin, no amount of expense-cutting downstream will save the net.

Why margin matters more than revenue

Chasing revenue while ignoring margin is the classic small-business trap. Doubling sales at a 5% net margin doubles your workload for a still-tiny profit — and often makes things worse, because low-margin growth eats cash (more inventory, more fees) faster than it returns it. Growing margin, by contrast, drops almost straight to your pocket. A 3-point net-margin improvement on your existing sales can be worth more than a month of frantic new orders.

How to raise a margin that's too thin

  1. Raise prices deliberately. A small, well-communicated increase usually lifts margin more than any cost cut — see how to raise prices without losing customers.
  2. Cut your true product cost. Buy materials in larger lots, reduce waste, or streamline the make process to reclaim labor time.
  3. Fix your worst products. Track margin per product and either reprice or retire the ones that quietly lose money — your bestseller by volume may be your worst by margin.
  4. Trim recurring costs. Unused software subscriptions and inefficient ad spend erode net margin every single month.
  5. Rebalance your channel mix. If one platform's fees crush your margin, shift effort toward the one that keeps you more.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good profit margin for a small business?

For a small product business, a net profit margin of 10–20% is solid, and 20% or more is strong. Below 10% is fragile — one bad month or price shock can erase it. Digital and low-material products can run higher; physical goods with shipping and marketplace fees typically land in the 10–20% range. Aim for a gross margin of 50%+ first, since every other cost comes out of it.

What's the difference between gross and net profit margin?

Gross margin is (revenue − cost of goods sold) ÷ revenue — profit on the product itself, typically 50–70% for sellers. Net margin is (revenue − all costs including fees, shipping, software, ads, and taxes) ÷ revenue — the bottom line the business actually keeps, typically 10–25%. Gross tells you if a product is worth making; net tells you if the business is working.

Is it better to focus on revenue or profit margin?

Margin, in most cases. Growing revenue at a thin margin multiplies your workload for little profit and often eats cash faster than it returns it. Improving margin on your existing sales drops almost straight to your pocket with no extra orders. Grow revenue once your margin is healthy — scaling a low-margin business usually just scales the problem.
