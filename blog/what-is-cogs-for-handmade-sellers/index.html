COGS for Handmade Sellers: How to Calculate Your True Cost of Goods

By The LedgerLane Team · Updated July 2026 · 6 min read

COGS — cost of goods sold — is the least glamorous number in your business and one of the most important. Get it right and every downstream decision (pricing, margins, taxes, which products to keep) gets easier. Get it wrong and you'll price off a fantasy. Here's what it actually includes, what it doesn't, and how to calculate it per product without a finance degree.

What COGS is

COGS is the direct cost of producing the specific goods you sold in a period. For a handmade seller that's three things:

  • Direct materials — everything physically in or on the product: raw materials, findings, packaging, labels.
  • Direct labor — the hands-on time to make it, valued at a real wage. (If you outsource part of production, that invoice is direct labor too.)
  • Direct production supplies — consumables used up in making it: glue, thread, a mold that yields a known number of units.
COGS is only the cost of what you sold, not everything you bought. If you bought $500 of materials but only used $180 of them on this month's orders, your COGS for the month is $180. The other $320 is inventory sitting on your shelf — an asset, not yet an expense.

What COGS is NOT

These are real costs, but they belong in operating expenses, not COGS:

  • Marketplace and payment fees (a selling cost, not a production cost)
  • Shipping to the customer and shipping supplies (often tracked separately)
  • Marketing and ads
  • Software, subscriptions, studio rent, tools that aren't consumed per unit

Why the fuss about the line? Because mixing these in makes your per-unit product cost look higher than it is and your operating costs invisible — and you'll make worse decisions in both directions. Keep production costs (COGS) and running-the-business costs (operating expenses) in separate columns.

Calculate it per product with a recipe

The cleanest way to get accurate per-unit COGS is a materials recipe: list each material, its cost per purchase unit, and how much of that unit goes into one product. A soap batch example:

MaterialCost per unitUsed per barCost per bar
Oils & butters$0.42 / oz4 oz$1.68
Lye & water$0.06 / oz2 oz$0.12
Fragrance$2.10 / oz0.3 oz$0.63
Wrap + label$0.35 / set1 set$0.35
Materials per bar$2.78

Add direct labor (say 6 minutes of active time at $20/hr = $2.00) and you have a COGS of about $4.78 per bar. Now you can price with confidence, and you know that a $9 bar keeps a healthy margin while a $6 "sale" price is cutting close.

Why per-unit COGS pays off everywhere

  • Pricing: COGS is the floor your price is built on — see our pricing formula.
  • Margins: revenue − COGS = gross profit; the truest early read on whether a product is worth making.
  • Inventory: knowing material-per-unit tells you exactly when to reorder before you run out mid-order.
  • Taxes: COGS is a real deduction; clean records mean you don't leave money on the table.

Frequently asked questions

What's included in COGS for a handmade business?

Direct materials (anything in or on the product, including packaging), direct labor (the hands-on time to make it, valued at a real wage), and production supplies consumed per unit. It's the cost of the goods you actually sold — not everything you bought.

Are Etsy fees part of COGS?

No. Marketplace and payment fees are a selling cost, so they belong in operating expenses, not COGS. Keeping production costs (COGS) separate from selling and running costs gives you accurate per-unit costs and cleaner margins.

How do I calculate COGS per product?

Build a materials recipe: list each material, its cost per purchase unit, and how much goes into one product, then sum to get materials cost per unit. Add direct labor (time × hourly rate). The total is your per-unit COGS, and it becomes the floor you price from.
