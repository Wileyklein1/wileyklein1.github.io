How to Take Good Product Photos at Home (No Studio, Just a Window)

By The LedgerLane Team · Updated July 2026 · 7 min read

Product photos are the single biggest lever on whether a listing gets clicked. A buyer scrolling search results judges your thumbnail in about half a second, long before they read a word of your carefully written description. The good news: you do not need a studio, a $1,500 camera, or a lightbox kit. You need a window, a few things you already own, and a repeatable process. Here it is.

Light is 90% of the photo

The difference between an amateur photo and a professional one is almost always the light, not the camera. Chase one thing: soft, even, natural light.

  • Shoot near a large window, ideally on an overcast day or in indirect light. Direct sun creates harsh shadows and blown-out highlights.
  • Turn off your indoor lights. Mixing warm bulbs with cool window light gives your product a muddy, off color that no editing fully fixes.
  • Bounce light back with a cheap reflector — a piece of white foam board, a sheet of paper, even a white towel — placed opposite the window to fill in the shadow side.
If your photos look flat or dingy, the fix is almost never a better phone — it's moving closer to a window and turning off the ceiling light. Free, and it works every time.

Your at-home setup, from things you own

  1. A sweep, not a cluttered table. Tape a large sheet of white poster board so it curves up the wall behind your product — this 'infinity' curve hides the seam and gives a clean, seamless background.
  2. Your phone on a tripod or propped steady. A stable camera means sharp photos and consistent framing across a whole shoot. A stack of books works.
  3. Tap to focus, then tap-and-hold to lock exposure. Most phone cameras let you set focus and brightness manually — use it so every shot matches.
  4. Shoot in the highest resolution your phone offers, and fill the frame with the product so buyers can zoom into detail.

The shot list every listing needs

Marketplaces give you multiple image slots for a reason — fill every one. A complete set answers the buyer's questions before they can ask:

  • The clean hero — product centered on the white sweep. This is your thumbnail; it wins or loses the click.
  • Scale shot — the item in a hand or next to a common object so size is unmistakable.
  • Detail / texture close-up — the stitching, grain, or finish that signals quality and justifies the price.
  • In-use / lifestyle — the product where it belongs, so the buyer pictures owning it.
  • 'What you get' shot — the full set laid out, essential for bundles and to reduce 'is this everything?' hesitation.

Editing that helps instead of lying

Editing should make the photo look like the product on a good day — not like a different product. Keep it honest:

  • Crop straight and square for consistency across the listing.
  • Brighten and set the white balance so whites look white and your product color is true. A wrong color is the #1 cause of 'not as pictured' complaints and returns.
  • Nudge, don't crank. Heavy filters and over-saturation set a false expectation the physical item can't meet — which turns into one-star reviews.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a professional camera for product photos?

No. A modern phone camera is more than enough for marketplace listings. What actually separates good photos from bad ones is soft, even light — shoot near a large window with your indoor lights off — not the price of the camera. Spend your effort on lighting, a clean background, and a full shot list before you think about gear.

What lighting is best for home product photography?

Soft, indirect natural light from a large window, ideally on an overcast day. Turn off indoor bulbs so you don't mix warm and cool light (which muddies colors), and bounce light back into the shadows with white foam board or paper. Avoid direct sun, which creates harsh shadows and blown-out highlights.

How many photos should a product listing have?

Fill every image slot the platform gives you. At minimum include a clean hero thumbnail, a scale shot showing size, a detail close-up, an in-use or lifestyle image, and a 'what you get' shot for sets. Empty slots make a listing look thin and lower buyer confidence.
