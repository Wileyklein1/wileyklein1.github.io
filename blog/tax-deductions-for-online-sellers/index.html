Tax Deductions for Online Sellers: What You Can Write Off (2026)

By The LedgerLane Team · Updated July 2026 · 8 min read
This is a plain-English overview to help you get organized — it is not tax advice. Rules, limits, and eligibility change and depend on your situation. Always confirm what applies to you with the IRS and a qualified tax professional.

Every dollar you legitimately deduct is a dollar you don't pay tax on — but only if you recorded it. Most online sellers overpay not because they're doing anything wrong, but because they never tracked the ordinary business costs the tax code specifically lets them subtract. Here's the map of what's commonly deductible for a product business, including the ones sellers most often miss.

The big, obvious ones

Start with the deductions that make up most of a seller's write-offs:

  • Cost of goods sold (COGS) — materials, direct labor, and production supplies for what you sold. Usually a seller's single largest deduction.
  • Marketplace & payment fees — every Etsy, Shopify, PayPal, and Stripe listing, transaction, and processing fee. These add up to real money over a year.
  • Shipping — postage and shipping supplies: boxes, mailers, tape, labels, filler.
  • Advertising — Etsy Ads, promoted listings, social ads, and product photography costs.
  • Software & subscriptions — the tools you run the shop with: design software, accounting tools, your email platform, website hosting.

The ones sellers miss

This is where money gets left on the table. Each of these is commonly deductible for a product business — verify eligibility for your situation:

  • Home office — if you use a space regularly and exclusively for the business, a portion of rent/mortgage, utilities, and insurance may be deductible (a simplified square-footage method exists). The "exclusive use" rule is strict, so confirm you qualify.
  • Mileage & vehicle — trips to the post office, craft fairs, and to buy supplies. Log the miles; the standard mileage rate can add up fast over a year.
  • Equipment & tools — cameras, printers, a heat press, a laptop, tables — the gear you bought to make and sell (may be deducted or depreciated depending on cost and rules).
  • Education — courses, books, and workshops that improve your business skills.
  • Bank & merchant fees — business account fees and separate merchant charges.
  • Professional services — what you pay an accountant or for legal help.
  • A portion of your phone/internet — the business-use share, if you use them for the shop.
The most-missed deduction is the one you didn't write down. An undocumented expense is a lost deduction — the IRS won't credit what you can't show. The habit of logging every business cost as it happens is worth more at tax time than any single clever write-off.

What you generally can't deduct

  • Personal expenses — anything not genuinely for the business. Mixing these in is the fastest way to trouble.
  • The value of your own labor — you can't pay yourself a deductible "wage" as a sole proprietor; you're taxed on the profit instead.
  • Inventory you haven't sold yet — materials become a COGS deduction when the product sells, not when you buy them (they're an asset until then).
  • Clothing you could wear anywhere — even if you wear it while working. Deductible clothing must be unsuitable for everyday use.

The key is clean records, not clever tricks

Notice that none of this requires aggressive maneuvering — just complete records. The sellers who pay the least tax legally are simply the ones who tracked every ordinary expense, sorted into the same categories the IRS Schedule C asks for. Track cleanly all year and filing becomes copying totals; track nothing and you'll either overpay or spend April reconstructing a year from a shoebox. Frequently asked questions

What can online sellers write off on taxes?

Commonly deductible business costs include cost of goods sold (materials, direct labor, production supplies), marketplace and payment fees, shipping and shipping supplies, advertising, software and subscriptions, and often home office, mileage, equipment, education, and a business-use share of phone and internet. The rule of thumb: ordinary, necessary costs of running the business are generally deductible — but only if you documented them. Confirm eligibility with a tax professional.

Can I deduct a home office for my Etsy shop?

Possibly, if you use a space regularly and exclusively for the business — the 'exclusive use' requirement is strict. When you qualify, a portion of rent or mortgage, utilities, and insurance may be deductible, and there's a simplified square-footage method. Because eligibility rules are specific, verify your situation with a tax professional before claiming it.

Do I need receipts for tax deductions?

Yes — documentation is what makes a deduction real. Keep records of every business expense (receipts, invoices, statements, and a mileage log), and sort them into consistent categories as you go. An undocumented expense is effectively a lost deduction, since you can't substantiate it if asked. Tracking expenses in the moment, all year, is the single most valuable tax habit for a seller.
